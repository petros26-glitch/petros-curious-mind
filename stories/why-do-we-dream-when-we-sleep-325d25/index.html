WHY DO WE DREAM WHEN WE SLEEP?

A betrayed kingdom, a dying king, and a god's mysterious punishment give birth to the ancient secret behind humanity's dreams.

Published 26 November 2026

WHY DO WE DREAM WHEN WE SLEEP?

The Curse of Tupemo

By Peter (Petros) Bamigbaye

An African Folklore

Long before the world became what it is today, when the names of nations had yet to be written and the boundaries between kingdoms were drawn by rivers and mountains, there existed a magnificent African land called Tupemo.

Tupemo was a land of extraordinary abundance. Its soil was so fertile that seeds rarely failed to grow, and its rivers flowed throughout the year, providing water for its people and their crops. Its forests were filled with fruits, its fields produced harvests beyond the needs of its inhabitants, and gold rested beneath its mountains.

People from distant lands spoke of Tupemo with wonder. They called it the Land of Milk and Honey, for no household went hungry, no child was left without care, and no stranger who approached its gates in peace was turned away without food or water.

At the centre of this prosperous land stood a magnificent palace, where a wise king ruled over his people. The king was wealthy, but he did not allow his riches to blind him to the needs of others. He believed that a king's greatest treasure was not the gold in his palace but the lives of the people who depended on him.

Under his reign, the people of Tupemo lived peacefully. Farmers worked their fields, traders filled the markets, and elders gathered beneath ancient trees to share stories about their ancestors and the god who protected their land.

For generations, the god of Tupemo had watched over the kingdom. The people honoured him through their traditions, believing that their prosperity was a blessing they must never take for granted.

Yet beyond the borders of Tupemo, there were people who heard stories of its greatness and desired more than the opportunity to visit.

They wanted to possess it.

One afternoon, a group of travellers arrived at the gates of Tupemo. They came from distant lands, carrying their belongings and wearing the dust of a long journey. Their leader approached the royal guards and explained that they had travelled far after hearing tales of the kingdom's extraordinary wealth.

"We have come in peace," he declared. "We seek only shelter, friendship, and an opportunity to see the wonders of your land."

The guards informed the king, who welcomed the visitors.

The strangers were given food, water, and places to rest. They were allowed to explore the markets, observe the farms, and admire the palace. The people treated them with kindness, shared their meals, answered their questions, and even invited them into their homes.

For the first few days, the strangers expressed gratitude for the generosity they received.

But beneath their grateful smiles, something else was growing.

They had seen the kingdom's gold. They had seen the fertile fields, the overflowing markets, and the magnificent homes. They began to imagine what it would mean to possess everything for themselves.

One evening, after the people of Tupemo had retired to their homes, the strangers gathered in secret.

Their leader stood before them.

"Have you seen what this land possesses?" he asked.

"We have," one of them replied. "There is more here than our people could gather in many lifetimes."

"Then why should we return to our lands with empty hands?"

Another visitor frowned.

"These people have welcomed us. They have given us food and shelter. How can we repay their kindness with evil?"

Their leader stared at him.

"Kindness will not make us wealthy. Friendship will not give us their gold. As long as these people live, everything we desire will remain in their possession."

He paused before delivering the words that sealed Tupemo's fate.

"We shall take the kingdom for ourselves."

Some of the strangers hesitated, but their hesitation did not last. By the end of the night, they had agreed to destroy the people who had welcomed them.

They would attack the kingdom, kill its inhabitants, seize its wealth, and claim Tupemo as their own.

They believed that once the people were gone, the land would belong to whoever possessed the strength to take it.

They did not consider the god who watched over Tupemo.

Nor did they imagine that the king they intended to kill would leave behind a prayer that would reach far beyond his grave.

The attack began before sunrise.

While the people slept, the visitors prepared their weapons and moved through the settlement. The first cries shattered the morning silence.

People rushed from their homes, struggling to understand what was happening. Parents searched for their children, neighbours tried to protect one another, and the royal guards hurried towards the palace.

The strangers attacked without mercy.

The people of Tupemo fought desperately to defend their families and homes, but the attackers had prepared for the violence they had planned. Soon, the streets that had once been filled with traders and children became scenes of terror.

The beautiful markets were destroyed. Homes were abandoned, and families were torn apart.

The king heard the cries of his people and ordered his guards to defend the palace. He did not remain hidden behind its walls, however. He watched from the palace entrance as his people fought for their lives.

He saw men stand against the attackers to protect their families. He saw women carry their children away from danger. He saw young defenders refuse to surrender even when the odds were against them.

And he understood that the kingdom he had spent his life protecting was falling.

Eventually, the attackers reached the palace.

The king's guards formed a protective wall around him. Citizens who had escaped the destruction joined them, determined to prevent the attackers from reaching their ruler.

"Protect the king!" someone shouted.

"We shall not abandon him!" another answered.

One by one, the defenders fell. Yet those who remained continued to fight.

The attackers were determined to reach the king, for they believed that killing him would complete their conquest.

At last, the protective wall broke.

The king stood surrounded by his enemies. His kingdom was burning around him, and the voices of his people were growing silent.

The leader of the strangers stepped forward.

"Look at what remains of your kingdom," he said. "Your people are defeated. Your wealth will be ours. Your throne will belong to another. There is nothing left for you to protect."

The king looked at him.

"You entered my land as a guest," he replied. "My people gave you food when you were hungry. We offered you shelter when you needed rest. We welcomed you without demanding anything in return."

He looked at the destruction around them.

"And this is how you repay us?"

The leader raised his weapon.

"We came for what you possessed. Now we shall have it."

The king knew that his final moment had arrived. But before the leader could strike, he lifted his face towards the heavens and called upon the god of Tupemo.

"O great god of Tupemo! Guardian of our land, witness to our lives, and keeper of the promises made by our ancestors!

"You have seen what has happened here.

"You know that my people welcomed these strangers in peace. We gave them food, water, and shelter. We allowed them to walk our land freely because we believed that kindness could build friendships between distant peoples.

"But they have repaid our kindness with murder.

"They have destroyed our homes, taken the lives of our people, and turned our beautiful kingdom into a place of sorrow.

"If my life must end today, let it end with my voice calling upon you.

"Do not allow this evil to pass without consequence.

"Let your judgment reach those who stand before me. Let it reach those who sent them, those who aided them, and those who will come after them.

"Let it reach their children, their descendants, their friends, and all who are connected to them.

"Let them discover a punishment that cannot be defeated by weapons or escaped by running.

"And when they close their eyes to rest, let them find no refuge from the judgment they have brought upon my people.

"Let their desires become their torment. Let their hatred confront them. Let their ambitions lead them into experiences they cannot possess. Let their fears follow them into the silence of the night.

"May the name of Tupemo never disappear without leaving a mark upon the world!"

His voice weakened. His body trembled, and the king fell to his knees.

The leader of the strangers watched impatiently, unable to understand why the dying man had chosen to spend his final moments speaking to an unseen god.

Then the king collapsed.

The king of Tupemo died immediately after making his final prayer.

For a moment, the world seemed to hold its breath.

The wind stopped. The birds fell silent. Even the attackers felt a strange uneasiness, although none could explain its cause.

Far beyond the reach of human eyes, the god of Tupemo had heard every word.

And his answer would change humanity forever.

The god was powerful enough to destroy the attackers where they stood. He could have brought down the mountains, swallowed their armies, or reduced their bodies to dust.

But he chose a different punishment.

He would not merely attack their bodies. He would enter the world of their experiences.

He looked into their hearts and saw everything they desired: wealth, love, power, recognition, and the satisfaction of defeating those they hated. He also saw what they feared: poverty, loneliness, humiliation, defeat, and the loss of everything they possessed.

Then the god reached into the mystery of sleep.

He created a doorway through which those desires and fears could become experiences. The doorway would open whenever the invaders surrendered their bodies to sleep.

That night, the first man lay down beneath the roof of a house he had seized.

He was pleased with himself. Tupemo's wealth was now within his reach, and he believed that nothing could take it away.

As he closed his eyes, the god entered his sleep.

The man suddenly found himself standing before an enormous palace. Its walls shone with gold, its halls were filled with treasures, and servants bowed whenever he passed.

He wore the finest clothes and possessed more wealth than he had ever imagined.

He walked through the palace laughing.

"Everything is mine!" he declared.

He sat upon a golden throne and looked down upon a kingdom that obeyed his every command.

He had never felt so powerful.

But when he reached for a golden cup resting beside him, the palace began to disappear. The walls dissolved, the servants vanished, and the throne crumbled into nothing.

The man opened his eyes.

He was lying on the floor of the house he had seized. His hands were empty, and the room was dark.

He had experienced extraordinary wealth, but he possessed nothing new.

Across the settlement, another invader was experiencing a different punishment.

He dreamed of a person he loved. They stood together beneath a beautiful tree, speaking as though the world contained no sorrow. He held the person's hand and felt a happiness that made him forget every trouble.

Then the person turned away and disappeared.

He searched desperately, calling out, but there was no answer.

He awoke with tears in his eyes.

In another house, a third man was running through a forest.

Something terrible followed him.

He could hear its footsteps behind him, but whenever he turned to look, he saw nothing. He ran until his legs ached. He tried to scream for help, but his voice seemed trapped within his throat.

Just as the unseen pursuer reached him, he awoke.

His body was unharmed. Yet his heart pounded, and he struggled to understand why he had experienced such terror.

The god had begun his punishment.

He had created a world in which a person could experience something without possessing it, love someone without finding them beside their bed, or face a terrible danger that left no physical wound.

And this world existed within sleep.

The following night, the punishment returned.

The invaders began to fear sleep, but their bodies could not remain awake forever. Whenever they closed their eyes, the mysterious world awaited them.

Some dreamed of wealth and woke to find their hands empty. Some experienced happiness only to awaken with a feeling of loss. Others found themselves surrounded by enemies, chased through strange places, or trapped in situations they could neither understand nor control.

They tried to protect themselves.

They sought the help of healers and elders. They performed rituals, changed where they slept, and searched for ways to prevent the strange experiences.

Nothing worked.

The punishment did not depend on where they slept or what they placed around their beds.

It followed them into sleep itself.

Yet the god's judgment did not remain confined to the original invaders.

The king had called for the punishment to reach their descendants, their friends, and everyone connected to them. And so the mysterious influence spread.

The invaders grew old. Their children were born, and their families travelled to distant lands.

As generations passed, the descendants carried the effects of the punishment with them. They married into other families, traded with distant communities, and moved across mountains and rivers, carrying the mystery into places where the name Tupemo had never been heard.

Eventually, the punishment reached people who had no connection to the original attackers.

It reached those who had never seen Tupemo. It reached those who had never committed the crime.

It reached the innocent.

And with every passing generation, the experience spread farther.

The god's punishment had escaped the boundaries of the people for whom it was first intended.

It had become a mystery that affected humanity.

But the strangest part was that the experience no longer brought only terror.

Some people began to dream of beautiful gardens and peaceful rivers. Others dreamed of celebrations, reunions, and journeys through magnificent places. Some dreamed of accomplishing things they had always desired.

Others dreamed of strange events that left them wondering where such experiences had come from.

The world within sleep had become complicated.

It could bring joy or sorrow, hope or fear, comfort or confusion.

And nobody understood why.

As generations passed, people began to notice something strange about the world they entered while sleeping.

A person could experience an entire life in a dream and still awaken to the same life they had before.

A poor man might dream of owning a magnificent palace. He could walk through its halls, sit upon its throne, and command servants who obeyed his every word.

Yet when morning came, he would return to his ordinary home.

A farmer might dream of fields overflowing with crops, only to awaken and discover that his work remained unfinished.

A merchant might dream of discovering a mountain of gold, only to open his eyes and find no treasure beside his bed.

A person might dream of travelling to a distant kingdom, meeting its people, and exploring its wonders.

But when they awoke, they would still be lying where they had fallen asleep.

This mystery troubled many people.

"How can I experience something so completely," one man asked an elder, "and yet possess nothing when I wake?"

The elder had no answer.

Others asked similar questions.

Why could a person dream of being loved but awaken alone?

Why could someone experience the joy of success without accomplishing anything?

Why could a person feel terrified by something that had never physically happened?

Nobody knew.

They did not understand that the mystery had begun with a king's final prayer.

They did not know that a god had created a punishment in which experiences could exist without becoming physical possessions.

The original crime had disappeared into the past, but its consequences remained.

The god had separated the world of dreams from the world people touched, saw, and inhabited while awake.

And humanity had inherited the mystery.

Not everyone welcomed the strange experiences that entered their sleep.

For some, dreams became a source of fear.

They dreamed of falling from great heights, being chased through dark forests, losing loved ones, or facing enemies they could not defeat.

Some awoke crying. Others awoke frightened, confused, or unable to forget what they had experienced.

There were people who dreaded closing their eyes because they did not know what awaited them.

Yet others looked forward to sleep because their dreams brought them happiness.

A lonely person might dream of being surrounded by friends. Someone who had lost a loved one might dream of seeing that person again. A person struggling through difficult times might dream of a future in which every problem had been solved.

When they awoke, the circumstances of their lives remained unchanged.

But the experience had been real to them while it lasted.

And this was the mystery that made dreams so powerful.

The same sleep that brought terror to one person could bring comfort to another.

The same mysterious world that allowed someone to experience extraordinary wealth could also leave another person running from a danger that did not exist in the waking world.

Centuries passed.

The once-prosperous land of Tupemo became a distant memory. Its magnificent markets disappeared, its royal palace crumbled, and the stories of its people were gradually forgotten.

The invaders and their descendants spread across distant lands. Their names changed, their languages changed, and their communities became part of the wider world.

But the dreams remained.

Children who had never heard of Tupemo began asking why they saw strange things while sleeping.

Adults continued to experience joy, sorrow, fear, and wonder in their dreams.

Some people dreamed of wealth. Others dreamed of love. Some dreamed of the dead, while others encountered people they had never seen before.

And when they awoke, they asked the same questions their ancestors had asked.

Why had they seen those things?

Where had those experiences come from?

Why could something feel so real while they slept, only to disappear when they opened their eyes?

No one could answer them.

The god of Tupemo had never revealed the truth to humanity.

Perhaps he believed that the world had forgotten its crime.

Perhaps he had allowed the punishment to grow beyond his original intention.

Or perhaps the dreams themselves had become a mystery even greater than the revenge that created them.

Only the god knew.

And the god remained silent.

Far away, beyond the memories of ordinary people, the story of Tupemo endured as a forgotten legend: the story of a land destroyed by greed, a king who died with a prayer upon his lips, and a god who chose to punish humanity through a world hidden within sleep.

And from that legend came the question that continues to trouble the minds of dreamers everywhere:

Why do we dream when we sleep?

Perhaps, according to the legend of Tupemo, every time we close our eyes, we enter the mysterious world that began with a king's final prayer.

Perhaps our dreams are reminders of a forgotten punishment.

Or perhaps the greatest mystery is not what we see when we sleep, but why we experience a world that disappears when we wake.

The people of Tupemo are gone.

Their king is gone.

The visitors who destroyed their land are gone.

But the mystery remains.

And every night, when humanity closes its eyes, the ancient mystery begins again.

THE END
